# Assumption and Counterfatuals in Causal Inference with Observational Data

**Contributor:** Songyea Lee

**Full citation:** Keele, L., & Minozzi, W. (2013). How Much Is Minnesota Like Wisconsin? Assumptions and Counterfactuals in Causal Inference with Observational Data. Political Analysis, 21(2), 193–216. <http://www.jstor.org/stable/24572654>

## 1. What the paper establishes

Different identification assumptions can lead to different estimates when researchers use observational data to infer causal effects. In this paper, the authors examine the effect of EDR on turnout to emphasize the role of assumptions in the estimation of causal effects with observational data. Regression and DID analyses suggest a positive effect, but the authors question whether these estimates can be interpreted causally because of concerns about the comparison group and the potential for unobserved confounding. Also, RD analysis using population threshold in Wisconsin finds no evidence that EDR largely increased turnout. The paper insists that causal claims with observational data depend on whether the design provides a credible counterfactual and whether its identification assumptions are plausible in the context of the study-not simply on which statistical method is used.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether a paper defines the causal effect of interest and states the assumptions needed to identify it from observed data.** An observational study cannot observe both potential outcomes for the same unit. The reviewer should check whether the paper explains how its assumptions connect the observed quantities to the causal effect it seeks to estimate.

**(b) If a paper uses regression or matching for identifying an effect from observational data, check whether it justifies the covariates and model specification on which that comparison depends.** In particular, assess whether the observed covariates plausibly account for factors related to both treatment and outcome, and whether the model specification is defensible. Also Matching does not itself eliminate the unobserved confounding.

**(c) For DID analysis, check whether a paper assesses the plausibility of the assumption that treated and control groups would have followed parallel outcome paths in the absence of treatment.** The reviewer should check available pre-treatment outcomes for patterns that cast doubt on this assumption and check whether the authors discuss other events that could have changed the treated and control groups’ outcome paths differently. Similar pre-treatment trends may support the assumption, but cannot establish that the groups’ untreated outcome paths would have remained parallel after treatment.

**(d) If a paper uses observational data and relies on a specification assumption to identify a causal effect, check whether the authors probe for hidden confounding by using sensitivity analysis, tests of predicted outcome patterns, or both, as appropriate to design and theory.** Sensitivity analysis examines how much unobserved confounders can change the inference and Pattern tests assess whether the results fit predictions from the causal explanation.

**(e) If a paper uses an RD design, check whether the authors justify the continuity assumption-that, without treatment, outcomes would have changed smoothly across the cutoff- and limit their causal claim to units near the threshold.** To assess whether the comparison is plausible, the reviewer should check whether relevant characteristics change suddenly at the cutoff and whether units may have sorted around it.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors first explain that "the individual-level causal effect of a law is unobservable"(p.194). They also state "Resolution of this problem requires an identification strategy: a set of assumptions that warrant inferences based on observable quantities”(p.194). They add that “ Identification assumptions thus bridge theoretical and observable quantities”(p.194). These support asking whether a paper specifies the assumptions linking its observed data to the causal effect it seeks to estimate.

**For (b).** The authors explain that “analysts must observe all the covariates that predict both the outcome and the treatment status”(p.195). They also state that regression and matching require the assumption that “the statistical model is ‘correctly’ specified”(p.195), and that “matching cannot correct for an ‘incorrect’ specification”(p.195). These statements support checking the covariate and specification rationale. In their EDR application, they add that “regression models do not account for why some states select into the EDR treatment and other states do not"(p.201). This illustrates why a reviewer should examine whether a regression analysis accounts for factors that are related to both the outcome and treatment. The authors’ matched analysis also shows a concern : “ if an unobserved covariate caused two identically matched voters to diﬀer in their odds of treatment by 1.2, then that would explain the estimated eﬀect”(p.204). ; Matching on observed covariates does not, by itself, rule out the hidden covariates.

**For (c).** The authors define DID's key assumption as "Identification, here, requires the expected potential outcomes for treated and control units to follow parallel paths in the absence of treatment"(p.195) and they explain that “This ‘parallel paths’ assumption is also nonrefutable”(p.195). In their EDR part, They suggest a critical threat to that assumption; “a competitive senatorial or gubernatorial race in the treated state could boost turnout, and the result of such an event would be attributed to EDR”(p. 201). Also they explain treatment effects detected before treatment that “cast doubt that observed diﬀerences are causal”(p. 196). These imply the importance of checking the assumption in context, considering other events that affect the trends, and evaluating available pretreatment outcomes.

**For (d).** The authors warn that “causal inferences based on specification assumptions are often not credible due to hidden confounders"(p.196). So, they introduce several ways to probe for hidden bias on p.196. For sensitivity analysis, they say "While the true value of Γ is unknown, we can try several values of Γ and see how the conclusions of the study change"(p.204). In the EDR analysis, they find “if an unobserved covariate caused two identically matched voters to diﬀer in their odds of treatment by 1.2, then that would explain the estimated eﬀect”(p.204). This shows how the estimate is sensitive to hidden confounding. For the pattern specificity; “one uses a pattern of confirmatory tests rather than relying on a single test”(p.196), they conclude that “the estimated diﬀerences do not fit the causal pattern we would expect”(p.205). These support checking sensitivity to hidden confounding and examining whether broader results fit the causal explanation.

**For(e).** The authors state that RD identification requires “the potential outcomes must be a continuous function of the score”(p.198). They explain the continuity assumption as “comparable or good counterfactuals for each other”(p.198). This supports checking whether the comparison around the cutoff is plausible. The authors also warn that “If there is a discontinuity in these baseline covariates, then the underlying identifying assumption in an RD is unwarranted”(p.199). In the EDR analysis, the authors implement density test “to check for evidence of manipulation in the forcing variable”(p.207). These support checking for covariates discontinuities and possible sorting around the cutoff. Finally, the authors state that RD “identifies a LATE for the subpopulation of individuals whose value of the score is at or near c”(p.198).

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** Observational research that makes causal claim with observational data. Check (a) apply to any such paper. Check (b) applies only when the paper suggests causal claims with regression or matching model through covariate adjustment. Check (c) applies to the research whose identification strategy is DID and that compares treated and control groups over time. The diagnosis of part (d) applies when the causal claim relies on the specification assumptions that may be affected by hidden confounders. Finally, Check (e) applies to RD designs in which treatment status or its probability changes at a known cutoff in a running variable.

**Does not directly apply to:** Experimental studies that identify causal effects through randomized assignment, or observational studies that use identification strategies outside the methods covered in this paper. A paper notes that It starts with an outline of only the key assumptions behind a number of popular approaches (p.193).

**Does not apply to:** Papers that make no causal claim, such as purely descriptive studies or correlational studies that do not interpret associations as causal effects of a treatment. Check (a)-(e) concern only identifying or assessing causal effects, so they should not be applied to those paper (p.194).

**Partial application:** Studies where treatment can affect other units. In footnote 3, the authors note that their framework assumes noninterference between treated and control units, but leave a detailed discussion of whether SUTVA is appropriate for a given study outside the paper’s scope (p.194). Apply check (a) to flag the interference assumption, but do not treat these checks as a full guide to assessing spillover effects. 

## 5. What failure looks like

A study claims a causal effect from observational data but never defines the effect or states the assumptions linking it to the observed comparison result. It uses regression or matching analysis without justifying the covariates and model specification on which that comparison depends, then considers matching as if it eliminates all the problems. A DID paper reports a causal estimate despite unexplained pretreatment differences or plausible events that could have changed the groups’ outcome paths differently. A paper treats its estimate as robust without considering hidden bias, or highlighting only the one result which align with the theory. An RD design paper reports a cutoff estimate as if it applied to all units without addressing continuity assumption, checking for covariate discontinuities or limiting its claim to units near the cutoff. 

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Dohoon Lee and Byungkyu Lee. 2020. “The Role of Multilayered Peer Groups in Adolescent Depression: A Distributional Approach.” American Journal of Sociology 125(6): 1513–1558. Using the Add Health data, the authors make causal claims about peer effects on adolescents depression and identify endogenous sorting, common shocks, and simultaneity as threats to those claims (p.1527). This makes check (a) relevant because the paper uses observational data to make causal claims. They also compare multivariate regression with within-school models (p. 1529), bringing the regression analysis within the scope of check (b). Their sensitivity analysis examines whether estimates change as observed covariates are added(p.1538). This makes check (d) trigger. Checks (c), (e) should stay silent because the paper doesn’t use DID or RD. 

**SHOULD NOT trigger:** Devah Pager. 2003. “The Mark of a Criminal Record.” American Journal of Sociology 108(5): 937–975. The author describes his study as an experimental audit approach (p.938). This uses matched pairs of black and white testers; paired by race, the criminal record condition is randomly assigned to members (P.946-947). Thus, the matched pairs are part of a field-experimental design, not an observational matching analysis. It means it’s not the kind covered by check (b). And also, this study’s causal identification does not rely on the observational data strategies covered by checks (a)-(e). So, those checks should not trigger.
